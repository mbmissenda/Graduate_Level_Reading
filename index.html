Skim Smart, Read Deep

You cannot read everything the way you read a novel — and you were never supposed to. This guide shows you how to decide, before you start, how deeply a research paper needs to be read, how to skim it well, and how to turn what you find into properly cited writing.

Section 1

Skimming isn't cheating. It's what experienced readers do.

Most new master's students arrive with one reading gear: start at the first word, end at the last, feel guilty about anything skipped. That strategy worked in undergraduate courses with a textbook. It collapses the first week you are handed five research articles, a systematic review, and a discussion post due Thursday.

Here is the part nobody says out loud: your professors do not read most papers straight through either. They read selectively and out of order, and they decide how much attention a paper deserves before they commit to it. That is a learned skill, not a personality trait — and it is one you are expected to build during this degree.

The evidence What research on research-reading found

Hubbard and Dunbar (2017) surveyed 260 undergraduates, postgraduates, postdoctoral researchers, and faculty about how they actually read scientific papers. Confidence and skill increased at every career stage — including between postdocs and faculty — and readers at different stages valued different sections of the same paper. Inexperienced readers found the methods and results sections hardest and, critically, undervalued the results section and the work of interpreting data critically. Experienced readers, by contrast, overwhelmingly recommended reading selectively within a paper rather than straight through.

The authors' conclusion is the reason this page exists: students need explicit, structured instruction in how to read the literature, not a vague instruction to "read the paper."

"These data highlight a need for structured support with reading scientific literature at multiple career stages, and for senior academics to be aware that junior colleagues may prioritise their reading differently." Hubbard & Dunbar, 2017, Abstract

So: skimming is not the lazy version of reading. It is a distinct skill with its own technique, and knowing when to use it is the difference between drowning in your reading list and running it.

But The honest limit

Skimming is a triage and orientation tool. It is not a substitute for reading a paper you intend to lean on. The rest of this page is mostly about telling those two situations apart — because getting that judgment wrong is how students end up citing a study that does not say what they claimed it said.

Section 2

Decide before you read: purpose sets the depth

The single highest-value habit you can build this semester takes about fifteen seconds. Before you open a PDF, answer one question:

What am I going to do with this paper?

Reading depth is not a property of the paper. It is a property of your purpose. The same twelve-page randomized trial might deserve three minutes of your attention or ninety, depending entirely on whether you are surveying a field or building an argument on top of it.

Skim or deep read? Work it out.

Answer three questions about the paper in front of you.

Q1Why are you opening this paper right now?

Q2Will you cite it — and for what?

Q3Does anything you'd write depend on how the study was done — the dose, the protocol, the sample, the comparison group?

Your recommendation will appear here once you've answered all three.

See it in your course

Same principle, two courses. Pick yours.

ISCI‑630a · Survey of Complementary Health Approaches

Scenario: This week you have readings spanning several categories of complementary approaches — a mind–body intervention, a natural product, and a whole medical system. Your discussion post asks you to compare how two of these traditions define and evaluate "evidence."

You are not making an evidentiary claim about whether any one intervention works. You need to understand each paper's framing: what the authors treat as a legitimate outcome, what they count as a control, how they describe the tradition. That lives in the abstract, the introduction, and the discussion.

Working skim is enough — for most of them

The exception: the moment you write a sentence like "this trial found a significant reduction in pain," you have crossed into citing a specific finding. Go back into that one paper and read the methods and results properly before that sentence leaves your draft.

ISCI‑631 · Introduction to Complementary and Integrative Health

Scenario: Nothing is assigned. You are asked to find sources that support your own work, so you run a database search and get back several dozen results. Nobody has vetted any of them for you.

This is where skimming stops being a time-saver and becomes the assignment itself. An assigned reading has already passed through your instructor’s judgment. A search result has passed through nothing but a keyword match. The screening is now your job — and it is a graded skill, not a preliminary to the real work.

Triage-skim everything: two to four minutes per result, keep or discard, capture the citation for anything you keep. Expect to discard most of them. Then the handful that survive, and that your argument will actually rest on, earn a full deep read.

Gear 1 on all of them Gear 3 on the few you cite

Watch for: predatory journals, opinion pieces formatted to look like reviews, and studies whose population has nothing to do with the one you are writing about. A triage skim catches all three in under three minutes — which is precisely why it is worth doing on all forty.

Section 3

Three gears of reading

Stop thinking of reading as on/off. Think of it as three gears you shift between deliberately. Every paper starts in first gear. Most stay there. A few earn third.

Gear 1 · Triage Skim

2–4 minutes

Purpose: decide whether this paper deserves any more of your life.

  • Title, journal, year
  • Abstract — conclusion first
  • Figure and table captions
  • Final paragraph of discussion

OutputKeep or discard, plus one sentence in your reading log.

Gear 2 · Working Skim

10–20 minutes

Purpose: understand what the paper claims and whether you can use it for context.

  • Everything in Gear 1
  • Last paragraph of the introduction
  • Design, setting, and sample from methods
  • Topic sentences in results
  • Limitations paragraph

OutputThree to five bullets and the full APA reference, captured now.

Gear 3 · Deep Read

45–90+ minutes

Purpose: you will cite, critique, or build directly on this paper.

  • Pass 1: Gear 2, to build the map
  • Pass 2: linear read, methods and results closely
  • Structured notes with page numbers
  • Your own critique written down

OutputCompleted note template you could write from a week later.

Rule Shift up, never assume

You can always shift into a higher gear, and you often should. What you cannot do is write as if you deep-read a paper you only skimmed. If you notice mid-draft that your sentence needs more than your notes contain — that is the signal to go back into the paper, not to soften the sentence until your thin notes can support it.

Section 4

When a skim is enough — and when it absolutely isn't

A skim is enough when…

  • You're screening search results for relevance.
  • You need orientation to a topic that's new to you.
  • The paper provides general background, not the evidence your argument rests on.
  • You're mapping a literature for a first-pass review and need breadth before depth.
  • It's a fact sheet, encyclopedia entry, or textbook chapter you're using to get your bearings.
  • You already know this literature and are checking whether this paper adds anything new.
  • You're checking whether a source is current, peer reviewed, and on topic before investing further.

Go deep when…

  • You will cite a specific number, effect, or conclusion.
  • The paper is the evidentiary backbone of your argument.
  • It's a systematic review or meta-analysis you're relying on — you need the inclusion criteria and the risk-of-bias assessment, not just the forest plot's bottom line.
  • The findings surprise you or contradict your other sources.
  • It's assigned for critique, close analysis, or journal club.
  • The design is unfamiliar and you can't yet judge its strength.
  • The claim involves safety, contraindications, interactions, or dosing. Never skim a safety claim.

Integrative health Why our field raises the bar

In complementary and integrative health research, the methods often are the finding. "Ginger reduced nausea" means very little until you know the species, the plant part, the extraction method, the standardization, the dose, and the duration. "Acupuncture was effective" depends entirely on the points used, the number of sessions, the practitioner's training, and what the sham control actually involved. "Mindfulness improved outcomes" is unreadable without knowing the program, its length, and the adherence rate.

Conventional pharmacological trials let you skim a methods section and still roughly trust the headline. Ours frequently do not. When the intervention is complex, heterogeneous, or poorly standardized, a skim of the abstract will mislead you — and you will write a sentence you cannot defend. Treat intervention characterization as a deep-read trigger in its own right.

Section 5

How to skim: the reading path

A skim is not "reading fast." It is reading in a different order, targeting the places where papers concentrate their meaning. Papers are built to a predictable template, which means the good stuff is always in the same few locations. Here is the path.

  1. Title, journal, year15 sec

    What is being claimed, about whom? Is this primary research or a review? Is the journal peer reviewed? Is it recent enough for your topic — and if it's older, is it a landmark or just out of date?

  2. The abstract — read it backwards60 sec

    Read the conclusion sentence first, then the results, then the methods sentence, then the background last. You'll know within seconds whether the paper is relevant, and reading in this order keeps you from getting absorbed by a persuasive introduction to a study that doesn't fit your needs.

    Ask: What did they measure, in whom, compared to what? If the abstract can't tell you, that's information too.

  3. The last paragraph of the introduction30 sec

    Almost universally, this is where the purpose statement, research question, or hypothesis lives. Skip the preceding four paragraphs of background — you can come back if you need them.

  4. Figures, tables, and their captions2–3 min

    This is the highest-yield target on the page, and it is the one inexperienced readers most often skip. Captions are written to stand alone. A results table will tell you the sample size, the comparison, the effect, and the confidence interval faster than three pages of prose.

    Hubbard and Dunbar (2017) found that experienced researchers rank figures and results as the most important part of a paper, while less experienced readers gravitate to the abstract and discussion — the two sections where authors are most free to put a favorable spin on what they found. Training yourself to look at the figures first is one of the fastest ways to read more like an expert.

  5. First and last sentence of each results paragraph2 min

    Results paragraphs are almost always topic-sentence-first. You can reconstruct the findings from openers alone, then drop into full reading only where something matters to you.

  6. The limitations paragraph60 sec

    Near the end of the discussion, the authors tell you where their own work is weak. Read it. It's free critique, it tells you what the paper cannot support, and it will keep you from overstating the study in your own writing.

Exit test Three questions before you close the tab

If you can't answer these out loud, your skim didn't work — go back to the figures:

1. Who did they study?  ·  2. What did they do to them, and compared to what?  ·  3. What did they find, and how confident should I be?

Techniques that make skimming actually work

Skimming a qualitative paper: the targets move

The path holds, but stops 4 and 5 change. A quantitative paper concentrates its meaning in the results table — the sample size, the comparator, the effect, the confidence interval. A qualitative paper concentrates it in the analytic structure: the sampling and setting, the named method (Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis, grounded theory, thematic analysis), and the theme table, code tree, or framework diagram.

So the figure to hunt for is the one showing how the data were organized, and the question to ask of the results is not "is this significant?" but "do the quoted data actually support the theme the authors named?" Section 6 walks through exactly this on a real course reading.

Use Find (Ctrl+F / Cmd+F) as a reading instrument, not just a lookup

Search deliberately for the terms that carry meaning: your own key concept, the intervention name, limitation, significant, adverse, adherence, dropout, funded. Each hit drops you into a sentence that's usually worth reading in full. This turns a 20-page PDF into six short reads.

Read the abstract twice instead of the whole paper once

When you're genuinely short on time, two careful passes through an abstract — one for the claim, one for the method and the numbers — leaves you better informed than one distracted pass through the full text where you retain nothing past page four. Be honest about which situation you're in.

Set a timer. Seriously.

A skim without a time box quietly becomes a full read, and then you've spent forty minutes on a paper you were only screening. Four minutes for a triage skim, twenty for a working skim. When the timer goes, decide: discard, shift up a gear, or move on.

Keep one reading log, not notes scattered across fourteen PDFs

One document, one row or short entry per paper: full citation, gear used, one-sentence takeaway, and whether you might cite it. Five minutes a week of upkeep saves you the experience — and every graduate student has this experience — of knowing you read the perfect quote somewhere and being unable to find it two weeks later.

Capture the citation the moment you decide to keep a paper

Not later. Later is when DOIs go missing and you end up reconstructing a reference from a half-remembered title. Copy the full reference, formatted, into your log the second the paper survives triage. This is a thirty-second habit that prevents hours of reference-list reconstruction at 11pm the night before a deadline.

Section 6

Worked example: skimming a real ISCI‑630a reading

Technique makes more sense applied than described. Here is the six-stop path from Section 5 run on an actual course reading — a qualitative study of why patients in Budapest turn to Traditional Chinese Medicine. Follow along with the paper open.

The paper

Zörgő, S., Purebl, G., & Zana, Á. (2018). A qualitative study of culturally embedded factors in complementary and alternative medicine use. BMC Complementary and Alternative Medicine, 18(1), Article 25. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12906-018-2093-0

  1. Title, journal, year15 sec

    Peer reviewed, open access, 2018. And the most useful word is sitting in the title: qualitative. That is an instruction. Do not come to this paper looking for effect sizes, p-values, or percentages — it is not built to produce them, and a reader who skims it hunting for numbers will close it convinced it said nothing.

  2. Abstract, read backwards60 sec

    Conclusions first: a person’s culturally shaped explanatory model runs through the entire patient journey; firsthand experience — their own or someone else’s — is the most trusted source of information about illness and therapy; and trust, built and lost, is a determining factor in decisions about both CAM and biomedicine.

    Then results: rather than sorting reasons into the usual "push" and "pull" factors, the authors organized everything along milestones of the patient journey.

    Then methods: medical anthropological fieldwork, January 2015 to May 2017, at four Traditional Chinese Medicine clinics in Budapest; 105 patients observed; in-depth interviews with 9 patients and 9 practitioners; Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis.

    You can now answer all three exit questions, and you are one minute in.

  3. Last paragraph of the introduction30 sec

    It closes by proposing "a panoply of CAM use factors based on the trajectory of various patient journeys with special attention to psycho-social and cultural context" (Zörgő et al., 2018, Background section). There is your purpose statement. You just skipped roughly three pages on how hard CAM is to define and categorize — genuinely interesting, and entirely skippable unless definition is your topic.

  4. Figures and captions2–3 min

    There is exactly one figure, and it is the whole paper. Figure 1 is the integrated code tree: biomedical diagnosis, biomedical consultations, biomedical treatment, philosophical congruence, and alternate "entrées" — each branching into sub-codes such as no cure, cure ineffective, side-effects, loss of trust, preference for the "natural," anti-drug disposition, preference for the "ancient," preference for holism, curiosity, prevention, and addiction.

    In a qualitative paper the coding structure is the results architecture. Ninety seconds with that figure hands you the entire finding, already organized, before you have read a line of the results. If you read nothing else in this paper, read Figure 1.

  5. Topic sentences in the results2 min

    The results headings mirror the figure, so you already hold the map. Skim the opening sentence of each subsection and drop into full reading only where it bears on your argument. The illustrative quotations from patients are italicized throughout, which makes them easy to locate — and if you intend to claim this paper found something, check that the quoted material genuinely supports it rather than merely sitting near it.

  6. The limitations paragraph60 sec

    There isn’t one. Look for it and you will not find it; the discussion runs into the conclusions with no section telling you where this work is weak.

    That is not a failed skim. That is a finding about the paper, and it changes what you owe your reader. The authors have not scoped their own claims, so you must. One city, one modality, nine patient interviews, and every participant recruited inside clinics they had already chosen to attend — which means this study can tell you a great deal about how those patients reason, and nothing whatsoever about people who considered TCM and decided against it.

What this skim does and doesn’t let you write

Here is the judgment the whole page has been building toward. A skim of this quality supports some sentences and not others, and the line between them is not about effort — it is about what the study design can carry.

You can write…

  • That decisions about complementary medicine are shaped by culturally embedded explanatory models rather than by evidence alone (Zörgő et al., 2018).
  • That in this fieldwork, patients frequently turned to TCM after biomedical treatment failed, offered no cure, or produced side effects they found unbearable.
  • That trust — and its loss — in the physician–patient relationship operated as a determining factor in therapy choice.
  • That firsthand experience, one’s own or another person’s, outranked institutional authority as a source of credibility for these patients.

You cannot write…

  • Any claim about how many CAM users think this way. Qualitative fieldwork does not measure prevalence, and was never trying to.
  • Any generalization to U.S. patients, or to modalities other than TCM.
  • Anything about whether TCM works. This study never asked.
  • A percentage, an effect, or a statistic of any kind — including the participant counts, which describe who was there, not a sample estimate.

The habit Name the study’s reach before you cite it

Most source misuse at the master’s level is not invention. It is quietly stretching a study past what its design can carry — turning nine interviews in Budapest into "research shows that patients prefer…" Before any sentence citing this paper goes into your draft, finish this sentence out loud: "This study can tell me ______, and it cannot tell me ______." If you cannot fill in both blanks, you have skimmed the paper but you do not yet understand it.

Section 7

Deep reading and note-taking

A deep read isn't a slower skim — it's a different activity with a different goal. You're no longer asking "what does this say?" You're asking "should I believe it, and what exactly can I claim on its basis?"

The two-pass structure

Pass 1 — Orientation (15 minutes). Run a working skim, exactly as in Section 5. You're building a map: what the question was, what they did, what they found, where the weak points are. Don't take detailed notes yet. Resist it.

Pass 2 — Interrogation (30–60 minutes). Now read linearly, with the note template open beside you. Because you already have the map, you'll read the methods and results with an agenda instead of passively absorbing them. You'll notice what's missing, which is usually where the interesting critique lives.

Reading note template — copy this

Full APA reference: write it now, complete, with DOI
Gear used: 1 / 2 / 3 — and the date you read it

Research question / purpose:
Design & setting: RCT? cohort? qualitative? where, when
Participants: N, who they were, inclusion/exclusion, who was left out
Intervention / exposure: be ruthlessly specific — preparation, dose, frequency, duration, who delivered it
Comparator: usual care? sham? waitlist? nothing?
Outcomes & measures: what instrument, at what timepoints

Key findings (my words): with page / table number for each
Quotable lines: exact words, in quotation marks, with page number
Limitations: theirs, then yours
Funding / conflicts:

How this connects: to my argument, or to another source
One-sentence takeaway:

Keep three voices separate — this is the plagiarism firewall

Most accidental plagiarism at the master's level is not dishonesty. It's a note-taking failure: three weeks later, you can no longer tell which lines in your notes were the author's words, which were the author's ideas, and which were yours. Mark them as you write, every time.

"  " Their exact words

Always in quotation marks, always with a page or paragraph number. If it isn't marked here, you can never quote it safely.

plain text Their idea, your words

A paraphrase. Still needs a citation — borrowed ideas require attribution just as borrowed language does.

[[   ]] Your own thinking

Your reaction, critique, connection, doubt. No citation needed — and this is the material that becomes your actual analysis.

Try it Write the takeaway with the paper closed

Before you finish a deep read, close the PDF and write your one-sentence takeaway from memory. If you can't, you didn't understand it yet — you recognized it. Recognition feels exactly like comprehension right up until the moment you have to write something.

Section 8

From notes to writing: citing in APA 7th edition

Your reading only counts once it shows up, accurately attributed, in your writing. This section covers the bridge.

Integrity Cite at the depth you actually read

This is the rule that connects everything above to everything below.

Never cite a specific statistic, effect size, or nuanced conclusion that you only saw in an abstract. Abstracts are the most optimistic paragraph in any paper. The results section is where the truth lives, complete with the confidence intervals, the subgroup where it didn't work, and the outcome that wasn't significant.

Never cite a source you haven't opened. If you found it cited in another paper, either go read the original or use a secondary citation — (Smith, 1998, as cited in Jones, 2020), with only Jones in your reference list. APA discourages this, so use it only when the original is genuinely unavailable.

If you can't find it in the paper, you can't write it. A sentence you can't point to a page for is a sentence that doesn't belong in your draft.

Paraphrasing well: three moves

Close the source. Then write what it means for your argument, in your own sentence structure. Reopen and check. If three or more consecutive words match the original, either rewrite it or quote it properly. Swapping synonyms into the author's sentence frame is still plagiarism — the structure is theirs too.

A good paraphrase is usually shorter than the original and points somewhere. If yours is the same length, you're transcribing, not thinking.

The same finding, three ways

All three are correct. Which you choose depends on whether the emphasis belongs on the finding or on the researchers.

Parenthetical — emphasis on the finding

Reading strategies become more selective and less linear as researchers gain experience (Hubbard & Dunbar, 2017).

Narrative — emphasis on the researchers

Hubbard and Dunbar (2017) found that readers at different career stages valued different sections of the same research paper.

With methodological detail — strongest for a graduate argument

In a survey of 260 undergraduates and researchers in the biological sciences, skill in reading the results section developed slowly across an entire academic career (Hubbard & Dunbar, 2017).

Direct quotation, source with no page numbers

The authors call for "structured support with reading scientific literature at multiple career stages" (Hubbard & Dunbar, 2017, Abstract).

Changed in APA 7 Three things students get wrong from habit

Three or more authors use et al. from the very first citation. APA 6's rule of listing all authors the first time is gone.

"Retrieved from" is no longer used before a URL, unless the content is designed to change over time and has no archived version.

The publisher location is gone from book references. No more "New York, NY:".

Quotation mechanics

Fewer than 40 words: run it into your sentence inside double quotation marks, with author, year, and page. Forty words or more: block quotation — start a new line, indent the whole block half an inch, no quotation marks, double-spaced, and the citation goes in parentheses after the closing punctuation.

At the master's level, quote sparingly. Quote when the author's exact phrasing is itself the point — a definition, a contested term, a claim you're about to take apart. Otherwise paraphrase, because paraphrasing shows you understood it and quoting only shows you found it.

Reference list entries

New page, the word References centered and bold, double-spaced throughout, hanging indent of half an inch, alphabetical by first author's surname. Article and book titles in sentence case; journal names in Title Case and italicized along with the volume number — but not the issue number.

Journal article with a DOI

Hubbard, K. E., & Dunbar, S. D. (2017). Perceptions of scientific research literature and strategies for reading papers depend on academic career stage. PLOS ONE, 12(12), Article e0189753. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0189753

Web page with a group author

National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health. (2021, April). Complementary, alternative, or integrative health: What's in a name? U.S. Department of Health and Human Services. https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/complementary-alternative-or-integrative-health-whats-in-a-name

Chapter in an edited book — pattern

Author, A. A. (Year). Title of the chapter in sentence case. In E. E. Editor & F. F. Editor (Eds.), Title of the book in sentence case (2nd ed., pp. 145–167). Publisher. https://doi.org/xxxx

More reference rules you'll actually need

Authors: list up to 20. With 21 or more, give the first 19, then an ellipsis, then the final author's name — no ampersand before it.

DOIs: always format as a live link beginning https://doi.org/. Don't write "doi:" or "DOI:". If an article has a DOI, include it even when you read the print version.

No DOI, from a database: if the article has no DOI and you found it through a library database, no URL is needed — end the reference after the page range.

Article numbers: when a journal uses article numbers instead of page ranges (common in open-access journals like PLOS ONE), write Article e0189753 where the page numbers would go.

Secondary sources: only the source you actually read goes in the reference list.

Personal communications: interviews, emails, and class lectures are cited in text only — never in the reference list.

Student papers: no running head is required. Check your assignment instructions, which override the manual.

Tool warning Citation generators and AI both get this wrong

Reference managers routinely mangle capitalization, drop DOIs, invent issue numbers, and misformat group authors. Generative AI tools will produce references that look flawless and do not exist. Check every reference against the actual source record before you submit — this is now part of the skill, not an optional extra. The thirty seconds you spend verifying is the difference between a polished paper and one that quietly falls apart under scrutiny.

Section 9

Self-check

Five questions. Answer them, then check — the feedback is where the learning is.

Q1You're screening fifteen search results to decide which four to actually use for your literature review. What gear?
Gear 1. Triage is exactly what screening means: two to four minutes each to answer "is this relevant?" Spending equal attention on all fifteen is how a literature search eats a whole weekend. Shift up only for the ones that survive.
Q2You skimmed a trial's abstract and want to write: "Participants receiving the intervention showed a 32% reduction in symptom severity." What do you do?
Verify first. A specific number is a deep-read claim, full stop. Abstracts routinely report the most favorable outcome, at the most favorable timepoint, in the subgroup where it worked. Option C is the more tempting trap: vaguening a sentence so your thin notes can carry it leaves you writing something you still haven't verified.
Q3Which part of a research paper do inexperienced readers most often skip — and experienced researchers rate as most important?
Results, figures, and tables. Hubbard and Dunbar (2017) found that less experienced readers gravitate toward the abstract and discussion — the sections where authors interpret — while experienced researchers go to the data. Making figures your first stop rather than your last is one of the fastest upgrades available to you.
Q4Your source is a study of an herbal preparation, and your paper will recommend it for a specific population. Which detail makes a deep read non-negotiable?
The intervention characterization — and the safety data. In integrative health, the methods frequently are the finding. "Turmeric helped" is not a usable claim without knowing which preparation, at what dose, for how long. And safety, dosing, and interaction claims are never, under any circumstances, skim material.
Q5Three weeks after reading, you find a striking sentence in your notes and can't remember whether you wrote it or the author did. What went wrong?
The three voices weren't marked. This is the most common route to accidental plagiarism at the master's level, and it's entirely preventable with a marking convention you apply every time. Option B fails in both directions: citing the author for your own insight gives away your analysis, and if the words were theirs, an uncited paraphrase of a direct quote is still a problem.
Section 10

Pocket checklist

Print this, or keep it open in a tab while you work.

Before opening the paper

  • What am I going to do with this paper?
  • Will I cite it — and for a specific claim, or for general context?
  • Gear chosen and timer set.

While skimming

  • Title, journal, year — primary research or review?
  • Abstract read backwards: conclusion, results, methods.
  • Last paragraph of the introduction for the purpose statement.
  • Every figure and table caption.
  • Topic sentences in results.
  • Limitations paragraph.

Before closing the tab

  • I can say who was studied, what was done, and what was found.
  • Full APA reference captured in my reading log, with DOI.
  • One-sentence takeaway written down.
  • Decision recorded: discard, keep for context, or deep read.

Deep read triggers — any one of these means shift to Gear 3

  • I'm citing a specific number, effect, or conclusion.
  • My argument depends on this paper.
  • It's a safety, dosing, interaction, or contraindication claim.
  • The intervention is complex or poorly standardized.
  • The findings contradict my other sources, or surprise me.
  • It's assigned for critique or journal club.

Before the sentence goes in my draft

  • I read the part of the paper this claim comes from — not just the abstract.
  • My notes mark exact words, paraphrase, and my own thinking separately.
  • Paraphrase checked: no runs of three or more identical words.
  • In-text citation matches a reference list entry, and vice versa.
  • I can say what this study can tell me and what it cannot.
  • Every reference verified against the actual source record.
